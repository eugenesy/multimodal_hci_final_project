# Style Guide: Writing an HCI Extended Abstract (TAICHI/CHI Format)

Based on a corpus of accepted HCI poster papers and extended abstracts, this guide outlines the specific language, tone, and structural conventions required to write a successful submission for venues like TAICHI 2026 or ACM CHI.

---

## 1. Core Tone and Voice

* **Empirical yet Accessible:** The tone should be highly academic and objective, but accessible enough that researchers from different sub-fields (e.g., psychology, computer science, design) can easily understand the core concepts.
* **Human-Centered:** Always frame technical interventions in terms of *human experience*. Do not just describe a system; describe how it shapes user perception, agency, or cognitive load.
* **Cautious but Insightful:** Because Extended Abstracts often describe preliminary or pilot work, avoid sweeping claims. Use words like *preliminarily investigates*, *suggests*, *initial evidence*, and *exploratory*.
* **Active Voice for Research Actions:** Use "We developed...", "We conducted...", "We evaluated..." rather than "A study was conducted...".

---

## 2. Structural Conventions

A standard HCI Extended Abstract (5-8 pages) typically follows this flow:

### A. Abstract
Keep it to 150–200 words. Follow a strict 5-sentence architecture:
1.  **Context:** The broad domain or problem. *(e.g., "Social media platforms increasingly rely on...")*
2.  **The Gap:** What is missing in current research or solutions. *(e.g., "However, evidence on how these designs shape in-the-moment agency remains limited.")*
3.  **The Intervention/Method:** What you built and how you tested it. *(e.g., "We conducted a controlled laboratory study (N=11) exploring...")*
4.  **Key Findings:** The most important quantitative and qualitative result. *(e.g., "Results indicate that...")*
5.  **Implications:** Why this matters for the field. *(e.g., "These findings suggest that...")*

### B. Introduction
* **Hook:** Start with a relatable human behavior (e.g., *doomscrolling*, *recalling a memory*).
* **Current Limitations:** Explain why existing solutions (e.g., screen-time locks, traditional digital search) fail.
* **Your Approach:** Introduce your specific intervention or concept (e.g., *Mnemonic Tracing*, *Sensory Interruption*).
* **Contributions:** Explicitly list your contributions, often as bullet points (e.g., a novel system, empirical findings from a user study, design implications).

### C. Related Work
* Group by theme, not chronologically.
* Use phrasing that bridges prior work to yours: *"While prior work has demonstrated X, it remains unclear whether Y..."*

### D. Study Design / Methodology
* Be highly precise. Clearly define your independent and dependent variables.
* **Key Vocabulary:** *Within-subjects design, counterbalanced, in-the-wild, semi-structured interview, baseline condition.*

### E. Results and Discussion
* **Triangulate Data:** Combine objective logs (time, accuracy) with subjective surveys (NASA-TLX, Likert scales) and qualitative quotes.
* **Quote Formatting:** Always contextualize data with user quotes, formatted with participant IDs: *"As one participant (P8) explained: '...'"*
* **Highlight Trade-offs:** Good HCI papers embrace nuance. Highlight tensions, such as effectiveness vs. user comfort (e.g., *“While heat was the most effective interruptor, participants found it punitive.”*).

### F. Limitations & Future Work
* Acknowledge the constraints of your pilot (e.g., *modest sample size, lack of in-the-wild testing, lack of a neutral baseline*).
* Propose concrete next steps.

## 3. Essential Vocabulary and Phrasing Toolkit

Use these proven sentence templates to build your paper:

### Establishing the Gap
* *"Despite growing concern around [Topic], much of the public and academic discussion still defaults to..."*
* *"Prior work has framed these decisions through the lens of [Concept], however..."*
* *"While [Technology] enables [Action], it also removes the [Quality] provided by [Alternative]."*

### Describing the Methodology
* *"To examine how [Variable A] shapes [Variable B], we conducted a controlled laboratory study..."*
* *"We employed a within-subjects design manipulating two perceptual attributes..."*
* *"The order of conditions was counterbalanced via Latin square to mitigate carryover effects."*

### Reporting Findings
* *"Our findings provide initial evidence that..."*
* *"At a reflective level, participants reported..."*
* *"Qualitative feedback revealed a consistent trade-off between [Factor A] and [Factor B]."*
* *"Interview responses converged on a clear distinction between..."*

### Discussing Implications
* *"These results suggest that [Design Element] may operate as a [New Function] rather than a direct replacement for [Old Function]."*
* *"This highlights an important tension between convenience and user empowerment."*
* *"Our preliminary findings point toward the importance of designing for..."*

---

## 4. Typography and Formatting Reminders

* **Acronyms:** Define acronyms immediately and use them consistently (e.g., Sense of Agency (SoA)).
* **Statistics:** Report stats cleanly inline: *(M = 4.56, SD = 0.99, p < .001)*.
* **Subheadings:** Use bold, descriptive subheadings in your Results section to summarize the finding (e.g., **"Habituation reduces the effectiveness of familiar cues"** rather than just **"Qualitative Results"**).
