## **SLIDE 2: THE PROBLEM (3 Photos)**
**[Duration: 45 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Look at these three scenarios.

**First: An elderly person shopping.** Pulling a trolly in one hand, trying to use a cane with the other. Their off-hand is fumbling. They can't feel where the screen edge is.

**Second: A surgeon performing remote surgery.** Their hands piloting a robotic arm through delicate tissue. One wrong tilt—disaster.

**Third: A drone pilot in a rescue operation.** Two hands controls different axis of the drone. If feedback is delayed or unclear, people die.

These aren't edge cases. These are real people, right now, struggling with the same problem: **one hand is busy, the other hand is blind.**

Your brain extends into the tools you use. A surgeon feels the scalpel. A musician feels the violin. But when your off-hand can't feel—can't get feedback—your brain can't adapt."

### Delivery:

## **SLIDE 3: WHY FEEDBACK CHANNEL MATTERS**
**[Duration: 30 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"There's a concept in neuroscience: **body schema.** Your brain doesn't think of your body as just bones and muscles. It extends your sense of self *into the tools you use*.

A surgeon's hands extend into the scalpel. A painter's hands extend into the brush. And yes—your hands extend into your phone.

But here's the thing: your brain only learns when it gets feedback. Touch, sound, vibration—these are the channels through which your brain adapts.

**Three researchers found:**
- Haptic (touch) improves your sense of where your hand is
- But audio (sound) can overload your brain without clear meaning
- And silence? Your brain is just guessing.

So the question becomes: *which channel helps your off-hand the most?*"

### Delivery:
- Use **the diagram** to visualize the feedback loop
- Make it concrete: "surgeon's scalpel," not abstract
- Emphasize the key insight: brain learns through feedback
- End with curiosity, not certainty

### Transition:
"To answer that, we ran a study..."

## **SLIDE 5: STUDY DESIGN**
**[Duration: 40 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"We recruited 25 people. They played a tightrope balancing game—keeping a ball on a virtual path, using only their non-dominant hand.

**Look at the cohort:** Mostly teenagers (13-15), balanced gender, 92% right-handed. We measured everything: falls, how close they got to the edge, their steering response.

The game had four difficulty levels—practice, easy, medium, hard. As it got harder, we watched what happened.

Three groups got three different feedback types: haptic (vibration), audio (beeping), or nothing.

Simple question: *does the feedback type predict who does better?*"

### Delivery:
- **Point to the experimental photo** as you describe the setup
- Make it relatable: "like a video game, but we're measuring everything"
- Emphasize the **cohort diversity** (shows it's generalizable)
- Keep it simple—one research question drives it

## **SLIDE 7: RESULTS — BASELINE CONFOUND & PROXIMITY**
**[Duration: 30 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Before we gave anyone any feedback, we had them play one practice round. Just the game, nothing else.

**Look at this table.**

The haptic group averaged **4.88 falls**.

The audio group averaged **0.33 falls**.

That's a **10-fold difference**. 

In other words, the audio group was *naturally better at off-hand control* before we even started testing. The groups weren't randomly assigned—they came in different.

This is the real discovery. Not 'which feedback works,' but **'baseline control varies dramatically by person.'**

This changes everything. You can't tell if audio feedback helped because the audio group started out already good. They didn't improve because of feedback—they were already ahead.

**This is the insight the whole study hinges on.** Baseline matters. Individual differences matter. More than which technology you pick."

### Delivery:
- **Point to the 4.88 and 0.33**—let the numbers speak
- Emphasize the **10× gap** (this is your "wow" moment)
- Use **conversational language**: "naturally better," not clinical
- Make the implication clear: this is a problem we have to solve

### Transition:
"So what does that mean for learning?"

## **SLIDE 8: RESULTS — LEARNING & STEERING RESPONSE**
**[Duration: 30 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"As difficulty increased, here's what happened:

At easy level, everyone improved. They figured out the path.

At hard level, everyone crashed. The task became impossible.

**All three groups. Same pattern. No modality difference.**

This tells us something crucial: **task difficulty is the real limiter, not feedback.**

You can give people the best haptic feedback in the world, but if the task is too hard, feedback doesn't matter. The ceiling is set by difficulty, not by technology.

Look at the steering response on the right—it shows how quickly people corrected when they got close to the edge. Again: no modality difference. Haptic, audio, or nothing—people corrected the same way.

**The story is clear: difficulty dominates. Modality doesn't.**"

### Delivery:
- **Trace both curves** with your hand to show they follow the same pattern
- Emphasize **"all three groups"**—repetition makes the point stick
- Use **short sentences**: "Difficulty is the limiter. Not feedback."
- This is your evidence phase—let the data convince them

### Transition:
"So now we have to ask: what does this actually mean for real people?"

## **SLIDE 9: IMPLICATIONS — MEASURE BASELINE FIRST**
**[Duration: 45 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Here's the honest conversation:

Everyone wants to say 'just add feedback and it works.' Surgery, drones, accessibility—'add haptics and problem solved.'

But we found something different.

We found that **people come with very different starting points.** Some naturally have excellent off-hand control. Others don't. A 10-fold gap.

And we found that **adding feedback doesn't erase that gap.** It doesn't automatically help everyone equally.

So here's the real insight: **Don't pick a feedback technology and hope it works. Measure each person's baseline first.**

For elderly people shopping: don't assume audio helps them. *Measure* if they can feel the phone edge. *Then* design feedback for their specific needs.

For surgeons: don't give everyone the same haptic glove. *Test* which surgeons respond to haptic vs. audio vs. visual cues. Customize it.

For drone pilots: same thing. Baseline matters more than which channel you pick.

One participant said something beautiful: 'It's a good training for elders or students with special needs.' Not because audio is magic. But because feedback helps *some people*. The art is figuring out *who*.

**The real solution isn't better technology. It's better *understanding* of individual differences.**"

### Delivery:
- **Be honest about the null result** — don't hide it
- Use **real-world examples** (surgery, drones) to make it matter
- Emphasize **the participant quote** — let real experience drive it
- Slow down for the punchline: baseline first, technology second
- Tone: reflective, not disappointed

### Transition:
"And that's the whole study in a nutshell..."

## **SLIDE 10: CONCLUSION**
**[Duration: 30 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Let me wrap this up.

We came in asking: *Does proximity feedback modality reduce falls?*

The answer is: *Not reliably. It depends.*

But that answer led to something better.

**We learned that baseline responsiveness predicts outcomes more than modality does.**

We learned that **task difficulty is the real ceiling.**

We learned that **individual differences are the dominant factor.**

And most importantly—we learned that **the research question shouldn't be 'which technology works.' It should be 'who responds to which technology?'**

This study said 'No' to modality mattering. But that 'No' was actually yes to something bigger: individual differences matter.

And that's pure gold for anyone designing accessible technology.

Thank you."

### Delivery:
- **Slow down** for this closing
- Make **eye contact** with audience
- Let pauses breathe—don't rush
- Own the null result as a discovery
- End with **confidence and clarity**
