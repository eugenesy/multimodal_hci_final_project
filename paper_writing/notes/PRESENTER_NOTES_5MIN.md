## **SLIDE 2: THE PROBLEM**
**[Duration: 50 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Think about the last time you tilted a phone with your off-hand—your non-dominant hand. It felt unbalanced. Awkward. Like you were fighting the device instead of controlling it.

Here's why: Your brain extends itself into tools you use. A surgeon's hands extend into the scalpel. Your hands extend into the phone.

But here's the problem: your off-hand isn't getting feedback from the phone. So your brain can't learn. It can't adapt. It's like trying to write with your off-hand in the dark."

## **SLIDE 3: PATHSENSE**
**[Duration: 50 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"We designed a simple game called PathSense. You're balancing a ball on a virtual tightrope. Just like in real life, you only use your non-dominant hand. Four levels of difficulty.

The catch: as you get close to the edge, the phone gives you a warning. But three different groups got three different warnings:

**Group 1: Haptic.** The phone vibrates your hand—harder as you get closer.

**Group 2: Audio.** The phone beeps—faster and higher-pitched as you get closer.

**Group 3: Control.** No feedback at all. Just you and the screen.

Twenty-five people. Four difficulty levels. The question was simple: which one helps most?"

- Make it concrete: "vibrates harder", "beeps faster"

## **SLIDE 5: THE DISCOVERY**
**[Duration: 60 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Before the study even started, we had all three groups play one practice round. Zero feedback. Just them and the game.

Here's what happened:

The **haptic** group averaged **4.88 falls**.

The **audio** group averaged **0.33 falls**.

Do the math. That's a **10-fold difference**.

In other words, the audio group was naturally better at off-hand tilt control *before we even tested our hypothesis*.

This is what we call a baseline confound. And it's massive.

Here's why this matters: if we'd just said 'audio beat haptic,' we'd be wrong. We wouldn't be measuring audio feedback. We'd be measuring that the audio group had better baseline control. That's not the story we want to tell.

This is the real discovery of this study."

- Point to the table as you talk through it
- Use your **tone** to convey importance

## **SLIDE 6: WHO BENEFITS & WHY IT MATTERS**
**[Duration: 60 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"This discovery teaches us something important: you can't just add feedback and hope it works. You have to measure baseline first. You have to control for the fact that some people start better than others.

And that matters for three groups of people:

**Gamers with motor challenges.** Think people with tremors. People recovering from stroke. Cerebral palsy. Right now, most games assume your hands work a certain way. But what if we could design games that *adapted* to your baseline? That knew your hand shakes and compensated?

**Older adults learning new tech.** Your grandparents didn't grow up with phones. Their fine motor control isn't what it was. They need feedback. Real, immediate feedback.

**One-handed users.** Amputees. People recovering from injury. People with one functional arm. For them, a smartphone isn't a luxury—it's essential. If we can make tilt controls accessible, we've opened up a whole new interaction paradigm.

PathSense is the testbed for all of this."

### Delivery Notes:
- **Make these vivid**. Use specific examples.
- **Paint a picture** for each group: what's their situation?
- Speak with **empathy**, not pity
- This is the **human impact** section
- **Pause** after each group to let it sink
- This answers your professor's question: "who benefits?"

### Transition:
"This isn't theoretical. This is about real people."

## **SLIDE 7: CLOSE**
**[Duration: 45 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Here's where we go from here: scale up to 50 participants—not 25. Test with adults, not just teenagers. Use real haptic devices instead of phone vibration. And get it into the real world, because phones get used in noisy buses, while people are walking, under stress.

That's the roadmap.

And here's my last thought: In science, we love positive results. We love it when something works. But null results—when something doesn't work like we expected—those are where the real learning happens.

This study came back null on the main hypothesis. But the blueprint we built from it—the discovery that baseline control matters, that you can't assume equal footing—that's pure gold.

Null results aren't failures. They're blueprints for better questions.

Thank you."

## **COMMON QUESTIONS & ANSWERS**

**Q: "Why didn't any modality win?"**  
A: "The baseline confound. We didn't control for that. Once we account for it, we realize we were comparing groups that started at different baselines. That's the whole point—you have to measure baseline first."

**Q: "Could you have fixed the baseline problem?"**  
A: "Yes. If we'd counterbalanced or had everyone do practice with the same modality first, we'd have leveled the playing field. That's going into the next study."

**Q: "How does this apply to actual games?"**  
A: "Right now, most games just add haptics and hope. Our work says: measure first, then add feedback. For accessibility, baseline measurement is essential."

**Q: "Why focus on non-dominant hand?"**  
A: "Because it's the weakest link. If we can make off-hand tilt accessible, we've solved the harder problem."
