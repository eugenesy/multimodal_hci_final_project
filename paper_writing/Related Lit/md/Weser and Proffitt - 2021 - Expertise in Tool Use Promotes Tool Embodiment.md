Expertise in Tool Use Promotes Tool Embodiment
Veronica U. Weser,a Dennis R. Profﬁttb
aDepartment of General Internal Medicine, Yale School of Medicine
bDepartment of Psychology, University of Virginia
Received 31 October 2020; received in revised form 18 March 2021; accepted 25 April 2021
Abstract
Body representations are known to be dynamically modulated or extended through tool use. Here,
we review ﬁndings that demonstrate the importance of a user’s tool experience or expertise for success-
ful tool embodiment. Examining expert tool users, such as individuals who use tools in professional
sports, people who use chopsticks at every meal, or spinal injury patients who use a wheelchair daily,
offers new insights into the role of expertise in tool embodiment: Not only does tool embodiment differ
between novices and experts, but experts may experience enhanced changes to their body representa-
tion when interacting with their own, personal tool. The ﬁndings reviewed herein reveal the importance
of assessing tool skill in future studies of tool embodiment.
Keywords: Tool use; Body representation; Embodiment; Training; Expertise; Tool incorporation;
Perception

can augment one’s capacity for action (Gibson, 1979). Expertise is attained through exten-
sive practice that yields an increased understanding of the effects one’s movement will have
on the tool and subsequently how wielding the tool will alter one’s interactions with the

the user thinks of the tool as an extension of their own body, the use of the tool is said

Within an embodied perception framework, spatial perceptions are scaled by those aspects
of the body that are relevant for intended actions (Profﬁtt, 2006). For example, a pebble
selected for throwing would be scaled to the size of one’s hand, whereas an open ﬁeld would
be scaled to the amount of walking required to traverse it. As such, the environment is per-
ceived in terms of one’s ability to act on it, with the available actions determined by the size
and shape of one’s body and whether it has been augmented by tools. If the environment
is perceived in terms of one’s ability to act, then objects that are within reach will appear
closer than objects beyond reachable space. The use of a tool to expand the area within reach
should thereby alter the distance perceptions of tool users, since the tool has expanded its
wielder’s capacity for action. Participants in a series of studies by Witt et al. perceived a
target that was within reach as closer when they held or imagined holding a tool, than the
same target when it was beyond reach without the tool (Witt & Profﬁtt, 2008; Witt, Profﬁtt, &
Epstein, 2005).
The ﬁndings of Witt and colleagues are representative of a sizable literature showing how
wielding handheld tools inﬂuences behavioral, perceptual, and physiological responses to
one’s immediately actionable surroundings, dubbed peripersonal space (Cardinali, Brozzoli,
& Farnè, 2009; Rizzolatti, Fadiga, Fogassi, & Gallese, 1997). Recent conceptualizations of
peripersonal space posit that it is a collection of ﬁelds proximal to the body where multisen-
sory information is integrated, allowing the brain to detect and anticipate potential interac-
tions between the body and external objects or events (Bufacchi & Iannetti, 2018; Hunley &
Lourenco, 2018; Serino, 2019). The concept of peripersonal space, and its mutability in the
case of tool use, emerged from a neurophysiological study of macaque monkeys. Two types
of bimodal neurons in the intraparietal cortex were discovered: “distal type” and “proximal
type” neurons. Distal type neurons respond to both somatosensory stimuli on the hand and
to visual stimuli near the hand. In contrast, proximal type neurons respond to somatosen-
sory stimuli on the shoulder and neck as well as to visual stimuli presented in the space
that the monkey can reach without bending at the torso (Maravita & Iriki, 2004). Iriki and
colleagues found that after training a monkey to use a tool to obtain food, the area where
distal type neurons responded to visual and somatosensory stimuli expanded from the hand
to include the tool. In addition, the proximal type neurons also expanded to respond to visual
stimuli anywhere within the monkey’s new tool-expanded reach (Iriki, Tanaka & Iwamura,
1996). This suggests that these distal and proximal bimodal neurons are responsible for

In lieu of directly recording from the intraparietal cortex of human participants, the cross-
modal congruency task is a commonly used research paradigm in the investigation of multi-
modal representations of peripersonal space (e.g., Maravita et al., 2002). In this task, partic-
ipants sit with both hands outstretched in front of them. Their task is to respond as quickly
as possible to vibrotactile stimulation that can occur on either their thumb or index ﬁnger of
either hand. Their task is complicated by a visual distracter stimulus that can appear near the
index ﬁnger or near the thumb of either hand. This combination of visual and tactile stim-

visual distracter stimulus appears at a location incongruous with the vibrotactile stimulation,
participants are much slower to respond to the vibrotactile stimulation. Participants are the
slowest when the visual distracter appears near the same hand that receives the vibrotactile
stimulation, but at different location on the hand (Maravita et al., 2002). It has been suggested
that this delay is caused by the conﬂicting activation of bimodal neurons that respond to both
visual and somatosensory information (Maravita & Iriki, 2004).
Given the ﬁnding that the distal type neurons of monkeys respond to visual stimuli sur-
rounding a tool tip following tool training, the canonical delay in identifying the source of the
vibrotactile stimulation at the hand should occur when a visual distracter stimulus is presented
at the tool’s tip. Indeed, participants who have practiced using a tool are slower to respond
to a vibrotactile stimulus delivered to their hand when a visual distractor appears at the tip of
their tool (Maravita et al., 2002). This suggests that, as in monkeys, active tool use expands
human peripersonal space to include the tool, resulting in a delay identifying tactile informa-
tion at the hand due to interference from processing the visual information at the tip of the
tool. However, as with monkeys, this expansion of peripersonal space requires training with
the tool: when participants passively held the tool, the crossmodal congruency effect vanished
(Maravita et al., 2002).
2. Expert tool users
In the tool use studies discussed so far, the tool tasks were relatively simple, requiring
touching a target (Witt et al., 2005) or crossing and uncrossing two held tools in a prescribed
manner (Maravita et al., 2002). Nevertheless, participants in control conditions who did not
practice with the tools did not demonstrate tool-use effects. To examine more complex tool
use, either longer training sessions are required (10–15 minutes is typical), or experts who
use their tool regularly in daily life, sport, or profession must be examined. While the tool
use effects documented in novice participants who undergo training prior to the experiment
do occur, they are also transient. In contrast, as we will show in this review, long-term tool
users often have more pronounced tool use effects that can be elicited even in the absence of
tool practice. These effects also persist and, in many instances, are dependent on whether or
not the expert uses their own personal tool in the experiment.

One interesting category of individuals to examine is the blind, who habitually and func-
tionally use a tool to expand their peripersonal space. Many blind people use a white cane,
which allows them to integrate auditory and tactile information as they touch the area imme-
diately in front of them with the cane’s tip. Serino, Bassolino, Farne, and Ladavas (2007)
demonstrated that blind cane users, experts in this tool’s use, generate a new representation
of multisensory peripersonal space in which the space around the tip of the cane has all the
multisensory integrative properties found for the space surrounding the hand. In this study,
participants were tasked with providing a verbal response to a weak vibrotactile cue that was
interspersed with a strong vibrotactile cue presented as a distractor. Both cues always occurred
on the right index ﬁnger and were always accompanied by an auditory stimulus. However, the
source of the audio could be either near the hand or near the tip of the cane. Regardless of the
source of the auditory stimulus, participants were told to ignore the distractor strong vibrotac-
tile cue and to respond as quickly as possible to the target weak vibrotactile cue. Blind cane
users responded faster to the target vibrotactile cue at the hand when the sound was presented
near the tip of their cane than when it originated close to their hand. In other words, auditory
information at the tip of the cane facilitated the detection of weak haptic stimulation at the
hand. This crossmodal facilitation effect was found without any tool practice session prior to
the experiment. This indicates that for blind expert cane users, the allocation of peripersonal
space to the tip of the cane for the purpose of multisensory integration is not only permanent,
but it may even reduce the effectiveness of multisensory integration at the hand relative to
integration at the tip of the cane (Serino et al., 2007).
For sighted participants who were novice cane users, detection of the target vibrotactile
stimuli was fastest when the source of the auditory stimuli was near the hand, rather than
the tip of the cane. However, following 10 minutes of blindfolded cane practice, sighted par-
ticipants’ reaction times did not differ depending on the location of the auditory stimulus.
This ﬁnding suggests, as in previous tool studies, that auditory peripersonal space expands to
include a cane following practice. However, the authors found that this expansion recedes to
pretraining levels after a day without cane use (Serino et al., 2007). Notice that in the absence
of daily cane use to navigate their environment, the crossmodal facilitation effect observed in
novice cane users was not nearly as pronounced as it was for blind cane users. As the authors
point out, for a blind cane user wishing to avoid collisions with external objects, the space at
the tip of the cane is much more important than the area around the hand. Thus, the altered
peripersonal space for expert cane users is a functional adaptation that ensures their ability to
traverse their environment without collision.
Another population of individuals who depend on daily tool use to maneuver in their envi-
ronment is paraplegic wheelchair users, individuals with lesions below the seventh cervical
spinal cord segment who experience sensory motor deﬁcits in their lower but not upper limbs.
To test how wheelchair embodiment affects distance perception, Scandola et al. recruited 20
wheelchair users and asked them to estimate the distance (in cm) to virtual ﬂags presented
at the top of ramps of varying lengths and inclinations. Participants viewed the ﬂags through
a virtual reality head-mounted display while seated in either their own personal wheelchair,
or a generic wheelchair of the type used in airports and hospitals (Scandola et al., 2019).

3. Body ownership
The notion that tools are “ready-to-hand” or seamlessly incorporated into the wielder’s
bodily dynamics appears in philosophy (Heidegger et al., 1962), but determining whether this
is the case empirically requires creative experimental designs. One method for studying body
ownership is to identify measurable differences in responses to a person’s own body as com-
pared to other stimuli. The body view enhancement effect provides just such an opportunity.
In the original study of this effect, participants were asked to lift their ﬁnger in response to
a projected light that appeared either on their ﬁnger or 5, 10, or 15 cm to the right or left of
their ﬁnger. Reaction times were up to 40 ms faster when the stimuli appeared on the ﬁn-
ger than anywhere else (Hari & Jousmäki, 1996). Subsequent reﬁnements of the body view
enhancement paradigm reliably show a more rapid detection of visual targets presented on
the hands, other body parts, or next to the hand (Dufour & Touzalin, 2008; Reed, Grubb,
& Steele, 2006) than the same target presented in the absence of a body part. Moreover,
visual stimuli presented directly on the hand are detected more rapidly than those presented
on wooden hand-shaped objects (Whiteley, Kennett, Taylor-Clarke, & Haggard, 2004). Even
more striking is the ﬁnding that this pattern persists when participants view video recordings
of visual targets appearing on hands, but only when the participant believes that the hand
seen in the video is in fact their own (Whiteley, Spence, & Haggard, 2008). This line of rea-
soning has been applied to studies of tool embodiment. In concert with the notion that tools
extend peripersonal space to include the area surrounding the tool, the body enhancement
effect also speeds the detection of visual targets presented on or near the functional portion
of a hand-held tool, but only after the participant has undergone a training session with the
tool (see Brown & Goodale, 2013, for review). Tellingly, wheelchair users respond equally
fast to visual stimuli presented on their own, personal wheelchairs and their upper body. This
response is faster than the response to stimuli presented identically on a generic wheelchair,
or even on their upper body when seated in a generic wheelchair (Scandola et al., 2019).

and action than are most tool embodiment paradigms.

4. Embodiment and measuring changes to the body representation
Another technique for assessing the embodiment of tools is the tactile distance judgment
task (De Vignemont, Ehrsson, & Haggard, 2005; Taylor-Clarke, Jacobsen, & Haggard, 2004),
in which participants must judge whether the distance between two points applied to one part
of the body is the same as the distance between two points applied to another body part
(the forearm vs. the forehead, for example). Since perceiving the size of an object touching
the skin depends on an implicit representation of the body (Longo, Azañón, & Haggard,
2010), changes to the body’s action capabilities following tool use, such as an increase in the
represented length of the arm following the use of a reach extending grabber, should cause a
change in the perception of the distance between two points applied to the forearm, but not
to the forehead, for example. Indeed, this is exactly what has been found: tools that extend
one’s reach (such as mechanical grabbers) inﬂuence the wielder’s representation of the length
of their arm, but not the size of their hand (Cardinali et al., 2009; Miller, Longo, & Saygin,
2014). In contrast, tools that expand the grasp of the hand, such as a device that lengthens the
wielder’s ﬁngers but not the wielder’s reach, speciﬁcally alter the implicit representation of
the size of the hand, but not the length of the arm (Miller et al., 2014). Interestingly, when the
same long-handled mechanical grabber tool is used to achieve a different functional end, such
as batting objects hanging from a string versus picking up and moving objects on the ﬂoor,
the resulting changes to the implicit representation of the length of the arm are also distinct:
Individuals using a grabber to reach and grasp a distant object make judgments indicative
of a longer perceived arm length, whereas those that use the same grabber to swing at an
object dangling in front of them make judgments indicative of a shorter perceived arm length
(Romano, Uberti, Caggiano, Cocchini, & Maravita, 2019). These ﬁndings demonstrate that
following extensive practice in laboratory settings, tools speciﬁcally alter the representation
of the body part that they are functionally augmenting, and that this alteration depends upon
the type of action implemented during training.
To further understand how the body is represented, Longo and Haggard developed a pro-
cedure to measure the implicit representation of the hand whereby participants place their
hands underneath a covered surface and point to the locations of 10 landmarks (ﬁnger tips
and ﬁrst knuckles) on their unseen hand (Longo & Haggard, 2010). Interestingly, healthy
adults consistently and signiﬁcantly overestimate the width of their hands and underestimate
the length of their ﬁngers (Longo & Haggard, 2010; Longo, Mancini, & Haggard, 2015). Fol-
lowing tool-training, Cardinali, Brozzoli, Finos, Roy, and Farnè (2016) determined that sticks
attached to the thumb and index ﬁnger caused the implicit representation of the thumb and
index ﬁnger to increase. Similarly, training with conventional pliers caused an increase in the
represented length of all of the wielder’s ﬁngers (Cardinali et al., 2016), again demonstrating
surprising speciﬁcity in the manner in which tool use alters body representation.

catching a baseball is a skilled behavior and the use of the glove may not have facilitated the
task as readily as a grabber aids a participant in picking up and moving objects far out of reach.
In contrast to the null ﬁndings for novices, players with 8 or more years of intensive baseball
experience had a signiﬁcantly decreased hand width and ﬁnger length of their gloved left hand
relative to their right hand, suggesting that when ballplayers accustomed to glove use are not
wearing their glove, they feel as if their hand is smaller. Indeed, one of the baseball player
participants in this study stated that “when I reach out to pick up a ball without my glove, I
feel like my hand is tiny and useless” (Coelho et al., 2019, p. 144). The authors speculate that
the perception that one’s hand is smaller may also confer a functional advantage by ensuring
accurate positioning of the center of the hand relative to the incoming ball. This is akin to
results of grasping studies in which participants employ a large hand aperture when they are
uncertain of the target to increase the margin of error and a smaller aperture when grasping
is easy and certain (Jakobson & Goodale, 1991). This explanation is in alignment with the
notion that perception, extended broadly to include somatosensation, informs people about
their opportunities for action. A baseball player wearing his own trusty glove feels as though
their hand is complete and can catch an incoming ﬂyball.
5. Conclusion
As in baseball, all sports require consistency in the successful performance of sport-speciﬁc
skills. Studies have shown that the apparent size of a target in a skill-based activity, such
as batting, is scaled to the performance of the athlete. For example, softball players with
better batting averages see the softball as bigger than players with lower batting averages
(Witt & Profﬁtt, 2005). Likewise, ﬁeld goal kickers who are kicking better perceive the dis-
tance between the uprights as larger (Witt & Dorsch, 2009). Although these examples do not
assess the effect of tools on performance, they allude to a similar pattern of results. Expertise
and skilled behavior alter aspects of perception that reﬂect the increased capacity for action
afforded by the expert’s level of skill. In target-oriented tasks, targets are perceived as larger
by more skilled actors (Lee, Lee, Carello, & Turvey, 2012; Witt, Linkenauger, Bakdash, &
Profﬁtt, 2008). In tasks requiring the judgment of some aspect of the body itself, experts with
extensive experience using a tool perceive their arm as longer, judge their hand to be smaller
in the absence of their tool, or are faster to identify targets appearing near their personal tools.
Perception relates the environment to people’s everchanging purposes and the embodied
means by which these purposes are achieved. Elsewhere, we have argued that people turn
themselves into walkers, throwers, graspers, and so on according to their purpose, and in so
doing, they perceive the world in relation to what they have become (Profﬁtt & Linkenauger,
2013). Experts, by the very nature of their expertise, are readily able to turn themselves
into a tennis server, badminton player, fencer, baseball catcher, or in fact live their lives as
a cane or wheelchair user. Therefore, when these experts are assessed using the experimental
