## **SLIDE 2: THE PROBLEM**
**[Duration: 45 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Think about the last time you tilted a phone to control something—a game, a browser scroll, anything. If you're right-handed and you're holding it in your left hand to tilt it, it probably felt unbalanced. Off. Like you were fighting the device instead of dancing with it.

This isn't a weakness. This is a design gap.

Your non-dominant hand—your off-hand—isn't as coordinated. That's biology. But here's the problem: most games and apps just assume you'll figure it out. They don't give your off-hand any help."

### Delivery Notes:
- **Act it out slightly**: pretend to hold a phone and tilt it awkwardly
- **Pause** before "This isn't a weakness" (reframe)
- Use conversational language: "dancing with it" is relatable
- Point to the image: "This is what I'm talking about"

### Transition:
"So why does this matter so much? It comes down to how your brain actually works..."

## **SLIDE 3: WHY? BODY SCHEMA**
**[Duration: 45 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"There's this concept in neuroscience called **body schema**. Basically, your brain doesn't just think of your body as your skeleton and muscles. It extends your sense of self into tools you use.

A surgeon's hands extend into the scalpel. A violinist's hands extend into the violin. When you use a phone, your brain treats it like an extension of your hand.

But—and here's the kicker—your brain only gets feedback through the tool if the tool is *telling* you something. If your phone goes silent when you're trying to tilt, your non-dominant hand isn't getting the feedback it needs to learn and adapt.

It's like trying to write with your off-hand in the dark."

### Delivery Notes:
- **Slow down** on the definition of body schema
- Use **hand gestures** to show extension (hand → tool)
- Make the analogy vivid: "writing in the dark"
- This is the **aha moment**—let it land

### Transition:
"Which brings us to our big question: what if we gave the phone a voice?"

## **SLIDE 5: PATHSENSE DESIGN**
**[Duration: 50 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Here's what we did: we created a smartphone game. Simple concept—you're balancing a ball on a virtual tightrope. You tilt the phone with your non-dominant hand. If the ball falls off, that's a fail. We call that a 'fall.'

The catch: as you get close to the edge, the phone gives you a warning. But the warning comes in three different flavors for three different groups of players:

**Group 1: Vibration** — the phone buzzes your hand harder the closer you get to the edge.

**Group 2: Sound** — the phone beeps faster and higher-pitched the closer you get.

**Group 3: Control** — no feedback at all, just what you see on screen.

We wanted to know: does haptic feedback—feeling it in your hand—beat audio? Or is video alone good enough?"

### Delivery Notes:
- **Point to the image** of the setup or game screen
- Use **rhythm** when describing the three conditions (one-two-three)
- Make it concrete: "beeps faster and higher-pitched"
- Emphasize the **research question**: which helps most?

### Transition:
"Twenty-five people, mostly teens, played this game. Four difficulty levels. Let me show you what happened..."

## **SLIDE 9: THE DISCOVERY (BASELINE CONFOUND)**
**[Duration: 1 minute]**

### What to Say:
"Before the study even started, we had all three groups play one round with zero feedback. Just them and the game. Same tightrope. Same rules. Same everything.

Here's what we got:

**Haptic group**: averaged 4.88 falls in that first round.
**Audio group**: averaged 0.33 falls.

Do the math. That's a **10-fold difference**. 

In other words, the audio group was naturally better at off-hand tilt control *before we even tested our hypothesis*.

This is what we call a **baseline confound**. And it's *huge*.

Here's why this matters: if we'd just looked at the final results and said 'audio beat haptic,' we'd be wrong. We wouldn't be measuring the effect of audio feedback. We'd be measuring the effect of the audio group being better at tilt control to begin with.

This is the real discovery of this study."

### Delivery Notes:
- **Point to the numbers** as you say them
- **Pause** after you reveal the 10× difference (let it sink)
- Use your **tone** to convey importance: this is the key insight
- Say "baseline confound" clearly—it's technical but important
- Emphasize: "This is the real discovery"
- This is the **moment where you show research rigor**

### Transition:
"Now, you might think this is bad news. It's not. This is actually *good* news. Here's why..."

## **SLIDE 10: WHY IT MATTERS (3 APPLICATIONS)**
**[Duration: 1 minute 15 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"This discovery teaches us three really important things:

**First: For game designers.**
If you're building a tilt-based game and you want to help people with motor control issues, you can't just slap haptic feedback on it and hope it works. You have to *measure their baseline first*. You have to control for the fact that some people start with better off-hand control than others. Otherwise you're flying blind.

**Second: For accessibility.** 
One-handed phone use is becoming more important. Whether you're someone with a disability, someone recovering from injury, or an older adult with declining fine motor control, your phone should work with your abilities, not against them. But to design for that, we have to understand the baseline. What can you do *without* help? And *then* where does feedback help?

**Third: For research in general.**
This study came back 'null'—no modality won. But that null result is actually *powerful* because it forced us to ask a harder question: why not? And the answer—the baseline confound—is a methodological insight that future researchers need to know about."

### Delivery Notes:
- **One point at a time**. Pause between them.
- Use the **structure**: First... Second... Third (easy to follow)
- Make each one **concrete and relatable**
  - Game designers → think of games they use
  - Accessibility → relate to people they know
  - Research → academic rigor
- **Don't rush** this section—your prof specifically wanted specificity
- This is where you answer "why this matters"

### Transition:
"So who actually benefits from this work?"

## **SLIDE 11: WHO BENEFITS (3 PERSONAS)**
**[Duration: 1 minute 15 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"Let's be specific. Three groups:

**Gamers with motor challenges.** 
Think people with tremors. People recovering from stroke. People with cerebral palsy. Right now, most games assume your hands work a certain way. Tilt controls? Forget about it. But what if we could design tilt-based games that *adapted* to your baseline? That knew your hand shakes and compensated? PathSense is the testbed for that.

**Older adults learning new tech.**
Your grandparents didn't grow up with phones. Their fine motor control isn't what it was. A teenager can tilt a phone by instinct. Your grandmother needs feedback. Real, immediate feedback. Our research shows that you can't just assume one type of feedback works for everyone. You have to *measure* it. You have to *test* it.

**One-handed users.**
Amputees. People recovering from injury. People with one functional arm. For them, a smartphone isn't a luxury—it's a lifeline. If we can make tilt controls accessible through better feedback, we've opened up a whole new interaction paradigm for people who otherwise can't access it."

### Delivery Notes:
- **Make these vivid**. Use specific examples.
- **Paint a picture** for each persona: what's their situation?
- Speak with **empathy**, not pity
- This is the **human impact** section—this is what your prof wanted

## **SLIDE 12: WHAT'S NEXT**
**[Duration: 30 seconds]**

### What to Say:
"To actually make this work, here's what we need to do:

**One:** Scale up to 50 participants per group, not 25. Bigger sample = more confidence in the patterns.

**Two:** Test with adults, not just teenagers. Is this age-dependent? Probably.

**Three:** Use real haptic devices—wrist-worn haptics, transdermal stimulation—not just phone vibration. A phone buzzer is weak. Let's see what happens when we use stronger feedback.

**Four:** Get it into the real world. Labs are controlled. But people use phones in noisy buses, while walking, under stress. Does PathSense work there?

That's the roadmap."

### Delivery Notes:
- **Quick and confident**. You've earned credibility by now.
- Number them (one-two-three-four) for clarity
- Make each sound **concrete and doable**
- This is the **momentum slide**—shows the work isn't done, but the path is clear

## **COMMON QUESTIONS & ANSWERS**

**Q: "Why didn't any modality win?"**  
A: "Great question. The answer is the discovery—we didn't control for baseline. Once we accounted for that, we realized modality effects are probably real, but they're smaller than we thought and maybe age-dependent. That's why we're scaling up to 50 participants."

**Q: "Could you have fixed the baseline problem by randomizing differently?"**  
A: "Excellent catch. Yes—if we'd done counterbalancing or had everyone do a practice round with the same modality first, we might have leveled the playing field. That's going into the next study design."

**Q: "How does this apply to actual games, like mobile gaming?"**  
A: "Right now, most games don't measure baseline. They just add haptics and assume it helps. Our work says: measure first, then add feedback. For accessibility specifically—people with motor control issues—baseline measurement is essential."

**Q: "Why focus on non-dominant hand?"**  
A: "Because it's the weakest link. If we can make tilt controls accessible for your off-hand, we've solved the harder problem. Your dominant hand works fine."
